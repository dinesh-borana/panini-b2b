# 03 — Setting Goals & Metrics Under Uncertainty

## Q: How do you set adoption targets with no baseline data?

**Short answer:** Tiered targets — floor, target, stretch — instead of single number. Floor = "something works." Target = "succeeding by reasonable benchmarks." Stretch = "category-defining." Better than single threshold because any number is arbitrary without baseline, and tiers let you tell a richer story.

**Long answer:** Single biggest mistake: conflating "users need this" with "users will use this." Need is necessary but not sufficient. You compete with habit, trust, awareness. A clearly better product can take years to reach 50% adoption — Google Pay in India is the classic example. So I treat industry benchmarks as gravity pulling toward realistic numbers.

**Story:** On Panini B2B my first instinct was 50% adoption in 60 days because buyers clearly need the catalog. But I challenged: is "they need it" same as "they'll use it"? It isn't. WhatsApp is deeply ingrained habit. Switched to tiered targets — Floor 15%, Target 30%, Stretch 50%. If we hit 35%, we know we did well. If 15%, we know something works but adoption is harder than expected.

**Don't say:** "I aim high to motivate the team" (vanity). "We'll figure it out after launch" (need anchor before launch).

## Q: Leading vs lagging indicator?

Leading moves before the outcome (predictor). Lagging moves after (the outcome itself). Repeat catalog visits = leading. Orders captured = lagging. Good design uses leading to course-correct before lagging tells you it's too late.

## Q: How do you avoid vanity metrics?

A metric is vanity if it can go up without product getting better. Total page views, total signups — easy to grow without value. Tie every metric to specific user behavior that maps to value. For Panini, "page views" = vanity; "% active WhatsApp buyers who visit twice" = real because it requires repeat engagement from a known user base.
